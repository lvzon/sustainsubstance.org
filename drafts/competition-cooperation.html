A tale of trust and cheating (part 1)

Why competition is overrated and cooperation is hard

Reading time: ca. 18-26 minutes

By Levien van Zon

Many of humanity’s greatest difficulties are coordination problems. They require us to collectively address issues that affect us all, directly or indirectly, although this isn’t always easy to see. As an example, think about the elimination of hunger and malnutrition. Most people agree that this is a worthy goal for humans to pursue. Yet in practice, the problem turns out to be a hard one to solve. Producing more food isn’t the solution, because collectively we already produce more than enough food to adequately feed all humans. The main problem is that the poorest people cannot access this food, due to economic and political inequality. And many people, especially in high-income countries and positions of power, actually profit from this inequality. Therefore, they resist changes to the status quo.

A similar dynamic is present in other large-scale coordination problems, such as reducing climate change, protecting ocean biodiversity, halting deforestation, fighting obesity or ending corruption and violent conflict.1 What these problems have in common is that solving them would benefit the majority, especially over time. Yet progress is repeatedly blocked by short-term, local interests. The “common good” is clear, but it often loses out to self-interest. If the rational, long-term choice is to prioritise the collective good, why is this so difficult to organise? Why don’t we just choose the “rational” option together?

In a previous article, I discussed one reason: much of our thinking (and thus our behaviour) is not rational. Instead, it is shaped by unconscious biases, feelings and narratives. It is hard for us to choose “rational” behaviour if our feelings and our social environment prioritise other needs. The most effective way to address this is by changing our social environment, especially the shared narratives that determine our collective priorities, and by extension, our personal behaviour.

The rationality of cheating

Unfortunately, changing narratives isn’t sufficient. There is an underlying, systemic problem that I haven’t discussed yet. If you regard humans as a group, it is clearly in our collective interest to prioritise the “common good”, through cooperative behaviour that benefits the group in the long term. Reducing conflict, malnutrition and climate change and protecting our resource base are all goals that clearly benefit humanity as a species. This benefits all humans on average and in the long term. But these “rational” common, long-term goals often conflict with the short-term goals of individual people and groups. In fact, for individual people, the “rational” approach is often to prioritise selfish personal interests over common ones. I will refer to this as the problem of cheating.2 Even if we decide as a group to cooperate on certain goals, it pays off for individuals to “cheat” on the collective goals in favour of personal ones. Provided that they can get away with it, of course.

This is not a trivial problem that can simply be overcome by collective willpower. The problem of cheating is a kind of natural law, and it is not just a problem for humans. In this sense it is somewhat comparable to gravity: it can certainly be overcome, but this does require a significant effort. Just as collectively wishing to fly is not sufficient for flying, collectively wishing to cooperate isn’t sufficient to attain collective goals. In other words, cooperation is possible, but it is hard. The good news is that while cooperating with others is challenging, it is also the main thing that makes us human. We are a uniquely cooperative species, and we come equipped with diverse and powerful tools that can reduce the problem of cheating.3 But we are by no means the only species that can do so. Somewhat less elaborate forms of cooperation can be seen everywhere in nature.

Common interests

Let me first define what I mean by cooperation. For the purposes of this article, I consider behaviour in a relationship to be “cooperative” when it foregoes immediate self-interest in favour of a greater common gain. Cooperation therefore involves a cost. At the very least this cost comes from forgoing maximum self-interest. But additionally, the more elaborate forms of cooperation require partners to explictly invest time or resources in their relationship. However, the cost tends to be worth it. Evolved cooperative relationships usually have a benefit for everyone involved. In biology, such a relationship is known as “mutualism”, because the benefit is mutual. A familiar example from nature is pollination and seed dispersal: plants provide animals with nectar, pollen, and edible seeds, while animals help plants recombine their genes and spread their offspring.

Cooperation (in the sense of forgoing short-term self-interest) is very common in nature, because it clearly benefits species in the long term. But how does it arise in nature, if the problem of cheating is such a big deal? Also, when we think of Darwinian evolution, we do not immediately think of cooperation. Rather, natural selection is usually associated with a different relationship, namely competition.

Emerging collectives

We tend to think of cooperation and competition as opposites, but this is not entirely accurate. It is perfectly possible for cooperation and competition to coexist, in fact it is very common. The most familiar example of this is probably our human economy. Already in 1776, the moral philosopher Adam Smith described the economic market as a decentralised cooperative system, with a a strong interdependence (resulting from the division of labour). There is also a degree of competition in such markets, of course, over finite necessities such as jobs and customers. But on the whole an economy is a cooperative social structure that benefits the entire society, because it creates possibilities and activities that would otherwise not exist.

In an earlier article I discussed so-called complex systems. These are systems in which interactions between components play a dominant role. I also introduced the concept of emergence, which is the appearance of new structures and behaviours, as a result of such interactions. A human economy is a system that emerges out of the interactions between individual people, or groups of people. Similarly, a natural ecosystem emerges out of the interactions between individual organisms or species. And our bodies emerge out of the interactions between trillions of individual cells.

Confusing concepts

Emergence as an abstract concept is difficult enough to grasp, but at least we experience its results first-hand. We all inhabit a body that exists and functions thanks to the cooperative interactions of large numbers of living cells. We can also experience the failure of these interactions, in the form of disease. Economies and ecosystems are harder to see and experience, but we can at least observe and measure their influence. But when we think or talk about the nature of interactions, such as cooperation and competition, things can quickly become murky and confused.

To illustrate the difficulty of these concepts, consider the following questions:

  1. If competition is so dominant in evolution, then why do we see so much collaboration and interdependence in nature?

  2. Mutualistic relationships are common in nature, but strong sociality is extremely rare and limited to only a handful of species groups, including ants, bees, termites and humans. Nonetheless, these groups have all been extremely successful. How can something so successful be so rare?5

  3. What is the “true” nature of human relationships? If we are indeed a super-cooperative and extremely social species, then why are corruption, conflict and exploitation so common?

These unfortunately are not easy questions to answer well, and I shall return to them at the end of this article.

Human glasses

One considerably difficulty when thinking about systems and interactions is that we tend to look at them through “human glasses”. We interpret the world through the lens of our own relationships, feelings and values. We depend on metaphors for thinking about abstract concepts, we translate the latter into similar human concepts that we are more familiar with. The word “cooperation” evokes images of people working together out of free will, perhaps having fun doing so. And “competition” evokes images of human gameplay, or worse, backstabbing or outright conflict. Moreover, we have opinions about such interactions, and we assign them moral value. We tend to see cooperation as “good”, at least within our own social group. Cooperation with opposing groups, on the other hand, is considered by many to be immoral. We may argue that selfish behaviour is a sign of strength, while prosocial behaviour is naive and associated with weakness. We might assert that competition is good because it eliminates weakness or inefficiency and drives progress. Or that it is bad because it undermines the cooperation that sustains human success, as well as the effective functioning of organisations.

Our experiences, beliefs and ideologies easily sneak into our thinking. Moreover, the history of a concept also influences the way we think about it. For instance, our thinking about competition has been strongly shaped by the historical development of two important scientific fields: economics and evolutionary theory.

Survival of the fittest?

Theories formulated in the 18th and 19th century still cause present-day people think of economies, natural evolution and even history itself as being driven primarily by competition—between individuals, companies, nations, populations and species. The underlying idea is that life itself is a kind of competition, in which the strongest or most successful individuals or groups “win out” over the less successful. Over time, this leads to “survival of the fittest”, a process by which less successful groups (e.g. tribes, companies, societies, populations or species) disappear and are replaced by more successful ones.

The idea that life is a competition which one can either win or lose is intuitively attractive, but it is not necessarily correct. Game theory distinguishes between the various possible outcomes of games, conflicts or other competitive situations. A naive interpretation of natural selection, as described above, is known as a zero-sum game. We call it zero-sum because one party’s win amounts to another party’s loss. Zero-sum thinking is widespread, for instance in the worldview promoted by competitive “strongmen” like Donald Trump. It is also a well-known cognitive bias, because real-world situations often aren’t zero-sum games.
Even in competitive situations, game theory distinguishes two alternative outcomes: In a negative-sum or no-win situation, everyone ends up worse off. This is the case in destructive conflicts (all-out nuclear war being an extreme example), but also in much more common race-to-the-bottom scenarios.
And a third possible outcome is the positive-sum or win-win situation, common in (semi-)cooperative competitions. Cooperative competition sounds like a contradiction in terms, but that is just because we tend to think of competition and cooperation as mutually excluding opposites (which is actually an example of zero-sum bias). I already mentioned the example of economic markets, first described by Adam Smith. According to Smith, the merchant looks only for security and gain for himself (i.e. his aim is selfish in principle), but the consequence is “to render the annual revenue of society as great as he can”. In other words, Smith describes the economic market as a win-win situation, even if that outcome is largely unintentional.

Emergent ecosystems

In modern terms, we could call Smith’s economic market a “human ecosystem”, a diverse and adaptive complex system of interpersonal interactions, with an emergent outcome that is (to some extent) beneficial to everyone. The emergence of such a win-win outcome is not obvious from the nature of the interactions themselves, especially given their often competitive nature.
My comparison of human economies to natural ecosystems is no coincidence, and isn’t superficial. Granted, natural ecosystems tend to be a lot more competitive (and violent) than human economies. But in both kinds of systems there is a strong interdependence. Like humans in a diversified economy, species in an ecosystem depend on each other, for food as well as for many other “services”. And in both cases there is a degree of competition (e.g. species compete for food, space or mates). And while many of the interactions in an ecosystem are negative (think of individuals eating each other), the net emergent outcome is positive for all species involved, especially in the long term. A functional ecosystem provides resources to everyone and takes care of recycling them. It is necessary for the survival of most of its members and it is self-regulating to some extent, adapting to changes in conditions and composition. It drives the process of evolution. Yet nobody designed the system to do all these things, or takes care of managing it. Functional ecosystems emerge from interactions, many of which we would consider “negative”.

Fitness vs. fittedness

Charles Darwin famously described nature as being “red in tooth and claw”, reflecting the violent nature of natural interactions such as predation and competition. But his theory of natural selection, published in 1859, has also been widely misinterpreted as “proving” the importance of competitive strength.7 To see why this is a misinterpretation, we must understand the nature of “survival of the fittest”.8 Natural selection is actually better described as “the survival of the well fitted”.9 We speak of “selection” because it is associated with a reduced reproductive success (at least relatively speaking) of those that are less well fitted to their environmental circumstances. It is important to realise that “well fitted” is not the same as “strong”. And the “removal” of poorly fitted individuals from a population happens over the long term and is not necessarily an active process, or one brought about by competition.

There are many different ways in which one can be “fitted” to circumstances, and many of these actually involve a little help from others. Some of this help is quite direct: We humans and other animals get assistance from the micro-organisms in our microbiome, that help digest our food and protect us from disease.10 This is a good example of mutualism, a close relationship from which both parties derive clear benefit. But much of the help we receive is less direct. Plants don’t just provide us with food and oxygen, they also help regulate the local climate. They hold on to water and reduce extreme temperatures, thus making our direct environment more suitable for life and increasing our “fittedness”.11 And predators aren’t merely “nasty dangerous animals that kill”, they also help control parasites, pathogens and competitors, which indirectly benefits us and many others.12

There are also many ways in which one can be poorly fitted to the environment. If circumstances are extreme, it is actually hard to be well fitted. When conditions are very hot, cold or dry, very few species manage to survive. Those that manage to do so may actually face little competition.13 When conditions are better, competition may increase. Those that are relatively the best adapted, with or without the help of others, will eventually crowd out others. This shows that a poor environmental fit can also be due to our relations with others: the negative influence of competitors perhaps, but also of pathogens, parasites and predators, or simply a lack of positive relations with potential “helpers”.

Escape from competition

Despite all this, when we think of natural selection, we mostly think of competition. In fact, Darwin himself emphasised the importance of competition, because it played an important role in his thinking.14 Moreover, Darwin’s England was a highly competitive society, at the height of the Industrial Revolution. Adam Smith’s ideas on free markets had by then become widely accepted, reinforcing a belief in capitalism and competition as engines of progress. Framing competition as a key driver of evolution made Darwin’s theory more intuitive and palatable to the public, helping it gain acceptance.

I’m not saying that competition is unimportant. It is a very fundamental interaction, which arises whenever two or more parties (e.g. humans, organisms or groups) share a common limiting resource. Elegant mathematical models show that under such conditions, and everything else being equal, the competitor that consumes the resource fastest will “win”. But the devil is in the details: by necessity such models usually assume simple relationships, limited diversity and a static environment.15 In real-life situations on the other hand, environments aren’t static and “everything else being equal” hardly ever applies.

Real organisms and species often have ways to reduce their competition with others, or to ameliorate its effects. They can relocate, switch resources, evolve and even coordinate with rivals or team up with potential helpers. In real ecosystems and economies, competition can become a force that eventually drives escape from competition, at least to some extent. Moreover, real environments are seldom static, so a competitive advantage today may become a disadvantage tomorrow. All this can greatly diminish the impact of competition. Unfortunately, these complexities are difficult to capture in elegant mathematical models. While more sophisticated models and simulations can demonstrate such effects, these are often hard to interpret or generalise.

Fascination and ideology

Simple theories on the other hand remain attractive, especially if they promise progress or success. Economists love to emphasise the elegance of competitive markets, and biologists love to show how competition can contribute to adaptation. We continue to be rightly fascinated by how positive outcomes can result from interactions that, morally, we might view as negative.

The emphasis on competition in Western thought also has an ideological background. The prosperity of Western democracies is closely tied to capitalist economics and ideals of individual freedom and achievement. Especially during the Industrial Revolution and later the Cold War, individualism, freedom and competition were elevated to Western core values. Collectivism and central planning on the other hand became associated with communism, and were viewed with suspicion. In recent decades, however, the problems of free-market capitalism and individualism have come under renewed scrutiny. At the same time, our understanding of cooperation in nature has improved. As a result, symbiosis has become somewhat of a fashionable subject.16

Living together

I have already mentioned that cooperative relationships usually carry a cost, but also have a benefit for everyone involved. In biology, such a relationship is known as “mutualism”, because the benefit is mutual. Symbiosis, however, is a different, broader concept. Somewhat underwhelmingly, symbiosis simply means “living together”. While it often involves mutual benefit, it also encompasses more one-sided and extractive relationships, such as parasitism. Nevertheless, mutualistic relationships are widespread in nature. As already mentioned, pollination and seed dispersal are one example. Another is the relationship between animals and their gut microbes. Similar partnerships also exist between microorganisms and plants. Soil-dwelling fungi and bacteria help plants access essential nutrients, often in exchange for sugars produced through photosynthesis. If this sounds a little like economics, it is because it effectively is an underground economic market, with some dynamics and mechanisms strikingly similar to human economies.17

A further well-known example of symbiosis is agriculture. Though we often regard agriculture as a human invention, many ant species were already practising a form of farming millions of years before humans began domesticating plants and animals. Moreover, agriculture illustrates the ambiguity of symbiotic relationships. Factory farming, for instance, may appear purely extractive, yet we depend on food crops, and from a genetic perspective they depend on us. This has led some to suggest that certain grass species may have domesticated humans, rather than the other way around.18

Relational problems

What we can already see from these examples is that mutually beneficial symbiosis is common, but that the relationships are often ambiguous with regard to benefit and choice or control. In other words, it can be difficult to draw a line between benefit and exploitation, or between collaboration and dependence. This, again, is partly a result of our “human glasses”, the fact that we view the world through the lens of human feeling and morality. We want to put things into neat categories. And we tend to think of cooperation as inherently “good” and, perhaps more importantly, as something that needs to be a choice. The issue is that if cooperation is a choice, then not cooperating is also a choice.

If non-cooperation is a choice, then this leads immediately to the problem of cheating, also known as the free-rider problem. There is usually a cost associated with providing benefits to others. Therefore, it is advantageous for individuals to try and forgo this cost, and to receive benefits without reciprocating. This “cheating” strategy is so effective in the short-term that it tends to outcompete and undermine mutualistic cooperation, even though—“rationally”—everyone, including the cheaters, would benefit more from cooperation in the long term.

Unfortunately, the problem of cheating is both real and very persistent, not just in human relationships but also in nature.19 As we shall see, it helps explain the inherent ambiguity in many symbiotic relationships. It also explains why large-scale social cooperation is rare in nature. Complex and intricate forms of cooperation are particularly vulnerable to cheating. Even ancient and successful cooperative systems still grapple with this issue, as seen in the occurrence of cancer in multicellular organisms like ourselves. Our cells clearly gain nothing from our death, yet our bodies still need to go through great lenghts to prevent individual cells from cheating the collective and going rogue.20 Similar challenges arise in every collaborative relationship.

Reducing freedom

This again raises the question: if cheating poses such a serious threat, how can strong forms of cooperation exist at all? It turns out that successful cooperation is indeed possible, and very powerful, but only if it manages to control the problem of cheating. There are several ways to achieve this, but all successful strategies for controlling cheating share a common principle. Remember that cooperation is vulnerable to cheating if cooperation is a choice, and therefore non-cooperation is also a choice. The key to robust cooperation therefore is to reduce the element of choice.

If we put on our “human glasses”, stabilising cooperation by reducing choice can easily sound like an endorsement of communist dictatorships, forced labour camps or patriarchy, but bear with me, this is not what I mean.21 Earlier on I described the concept of emergence as the appearance of new structures and behaviours out of interactions. However, as Terrence Deacon and Jeremy Sherman argue, it may be more useful to view emergence as a process of removal and constraint. For new structural order to emerge in a system, its component parts must lose some of their original degrees of freedom. If those parts retained complete freedom, it would be unlikely to yield any coherent order, let alone new, functional behaviour.22

All forms of emergence require some restriction of freedom for their components, enabling their interaction to generate functional behaviour rather than chaos. We would not exist as functioning individuals if the cells that make up our body would be free to do whatever they pleased. Instead, the behaviour of our cells is strongly constrained by genetic programmes, by physics and by our immune system. The human immune system not only protects us against pathogens, but it also removes potential cancer cells, at least most of the time.
Thankfully we humans have a lot more freedom than the cells in our body do. Nonetheless, autonomous humans in a “free market” society are still not truly free—they follow laws, traditions, and moral norms, and rely on institutions.23 Without such constraints, the result would be disorder, not a functional economy or society that benefits its participants.
The “wild” organisms in a natural ecosystem are of course not bound by morality or tradition, but even they are not entirely free; they follow innate behavioural patterns and they depend on and are constrained by others. Function always demands some kind of order, and order requires some restriction of freedom.

Mutual control

For cooperative systems to work, this restriction of freedom must take a specific form: it must prevent any single member from benefiting too much at the expense of others in the relationship. The simplest way to achieve this (though not always sufficient) is through co-dependence, also known as obligate symbiosis. After all, if two parties depend entirely on each other for survival, cheating on the relationship becomes far more difficult.24 Another strategy is the policing of non-cooperation. Our immune system removes cells that misbehave, and all human societies have mechanisms to punish antisocial behaviour.27

It is intuitively clear how either mutual dependence or “policing” to punish non-cooperation can prevent or limit cheating. Both mechanisms leave cooperators with little choice but to cooperate. Yet even under less restrictive conditions, nature exhibits stable, mutually beneficial cooperation that appears to arise from free choice. What stops either partner from exploiting the other?
In fact, wherever we observe a mutually beneficial relationship between non-dependent partners in nature, the same interaction can often be described as “mutual parasitism”. Each party seeks to maximise its own benefit, but some power balance or external constraint keeps them in check.25

Consider the complex relationship between plants and mycorrhizal fungi. One could view this as mutual parasitism: fungi “infecting” plant roots to access sugars, or trees “domesticating” fungi to obtain more nutrients. Both perspectives have some merit, though neither is entirely accurate. In fact both plants and fungi coordinate with each other and have some control over their relationship. They can survive without each other and they can choose to withhold or reduce their contribution to the relationship. If one partner “cheats” (which occasionally happens), the other can respond by withdrawing its own contribution. In short, cheating carries negative consequences, which severely limits the potential success of cheaters. While cheating remains possible, a balance of power prevents one partner from gaining too much benefit at the other’s expense.26

Stability through dependence

Viewing a mutually beneficial relationship as “mutual parasitism” may seem a cynical take on cooperation. Yet it is only cynical from a human perspective, in which we tend to think of cooperation as a free and moral choice. This perhaps reveals more about the cooperative roots of human morality than about cooperation itself.
From a non-human standpoint, cooperation is a dependence relationship, which is more stable if there is less choice. It is beneficial in part because it unlocks new opportunities. To achieve this, however, it must restrict the freedom of all involved to act selfishly at others’ expense. This restriction then enables the emergence of cooperative systems that enrich the world and, in the long run, leave everyone better off.

In human societies, restricting selfish behaviour mainly means preventing the (excessive) exploitation of others or of “common goods”. How such restrictions are established, stabilised, and whether they are “fair” will be the subject of my next article.

Revisiting relationships

As promised, I will now return to the questions I posed earlier, to illustrate that cooperation and competition are difficult and often confusing subjects. We can finally provide some answers:

  1. If competition is so dominant in evolution, then why do we see so much collaboration and interdependence in nature? The short answer is that symbioses are common in nature because they pay off. Mutually beneficial cooperation creates win-win scenarios. Moreover, it can coexist with competition.

  2. Strong sociality is extremely rare and limited to only a handful of species groups, which have been extremely successful. How can something so successful be so rare? Not all types of collaboration are equally strong, and stronger forms of cooperation are more powerful but also more vulnerable to the problem of cheating. Intricate forms of cooperation can be extremely effective, if they manage to evolve and persist. This in turn requires the evolution of mechanisms to suppress cheating, which clearly isn’t easy.

  3. If we humans are indeed a super-cooperative and extremely social species, then why are corruption, conflict and exploitation so common? This, again, involves the problem of cheating: corruption can be an extremely successful strategy, if you can get away with it. And many social systems let us get away with it. Conflict and exploitation however, are more complicated. Much human conflict is actually the result of cooperation within groups, combined with competition between groups. This is a hairy issue that I will address later.

Competition is not unimportant. We cannot understand ecosystems, economies, or evolution without invoking and understanding competition. But competition is also “easy”, in the sense that a competitive setting occurs wherever there are shared, limiting resources. Competition is therefore the baseline expectation in growing systems, which are always eventually limited by some resource such as space, energy or raw material.

What makes life remarkable is that it does not always take the easy path. Life is a constant struggle against decay and disorder, and thus an active, effortful process of organisation. It is therefore unsurprising that living beings have evolved ways to escape or reduce the constraints of competition. Indeed, much of the richness of nature and human culture arises from this very effort. Even in nature, competition can occur together with coordination, dependence, exploitation and cooperation. In fact, this is what generates much of the diversity and complexity we see in natural ecosystems. If competition were the only game in town, nature would look very different, and human culture would not exist.

Escape from cheating

As we have seen, cooperation is often the best long-term strategy for everyone, but self-interested cheating tends to pay off for individuals in the short term. This is not an easy problem to solve, yet life has found ways to mitigate it to some extent, and so has human culture.

Stabilising cooperation requires limiting the freedom to act selfishly at the expense of others. Such restrictions can take many forms. More intimate and complex forms of cooperation demand stronger and more varied mechanisms to suppress cheating. As groups grow larger and more diverse, as in the evolution of human societies, cooperation becomes more difficult. Human cooperation relies on a combination of many stabilising mechanisms and structures. These include co-dependence, social feelings, trust, morality, punishment of cheating and several kinds of institutions. None of these are perfect, which is why human societies remain plagued by corruption, violence, inequality and “wicked” coordination problems. Yet this is not a situation we need to accept. We can improve the structures and mechanisms that underpin human cooperation, if we understand how they work and when they sometimes fail. This is what I will explore in the next article.
