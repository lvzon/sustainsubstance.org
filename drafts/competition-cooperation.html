A tale of trust and cheating (part 1)

Why competition is overrated and cooperation is hard

Reading time: ca. 24 minutes

By Levien van Zon

Humans are not merely a bunch of isolated individuals, free to act as they please. If we were, society could not function and we would not survive long. Instead, we are a highly cooperative species, and this has made us very successful. Yet this very nature also creates its own challenges. Many of humanity’s greatest difficulties are, in fact, coordination problems: they require us to collectively address issues that affect us all, either positively or negatively.
Most would agree, for instance, that eliminating hunger and malnutrition is a worthy goal. But producing more or better food alone does not solve the problem, as the poorest would still lack access. To really end hunger, we must also tackle poverty and inequality, and this is a far more complex task. The poor would certainly profit from this, but a significant part of the world population actually benefits from the current, unequal status quo. Understandably, people resist change that would make them worse off.

Climate change presents a similar dilemma. Transitioning away from fossil fuels is technically feasible, yet doing so would make many goods and services more expensive, at least in the short term. While preventing further climate degradation serves the long-term interests of most, the necessary actions conflict with the short-term interests of a significant portion of the population.
Further examples are not hard to find: protecting ocean biodiversity, halting deforestation, reducing factory-farmed meat consumption, ensuring access to clean water, fighting obesity or ending corruption and violent conflict. These “wicked” coordination problems share a common thread: solving them would benefit the majority, especially over time, yet progress is repeatedly blocked by short-term, local interests. The “common good” is clear, but it often loses out to self-interest. If the rational, long-term choice is to prioritise the collective good, why is this so difficult to organise? Why don’t we just choose the “rational” option together?

In a previous article, I discussed one reason: much of our thinking (and thus our behaviour) is not rational. Instead, it is shaped by unconscious biases, feelings and narratives. But unfortunately the issue runs deeper than mere human “irrationality”. There is also a systemic problem: It turns out that even the rational thing to do for individuals is often to prioritise short-term personal gain over long-term collective benefit. This tends to yield better outcomes for the individual, at the expense of the group. This “problem of cheating” turns out to be an inherent problem for all cooperative groups.1 It makes stable, effective cooperation very difficult. This may seem like bad news for humanity (as well as for many other species). However, the good news is that there are ways to overcome this problem, and humans have been remarkably successful at doing so since we diverged from our primate ancestors. Cooperation may be challenging, but it is also the main thing that makes us human.

There is a lot of confusion surrounding the subject of cooperation and its seeming opposite, competition. As we shall see, this has to do with the history of Western thinking about economics, evolution and individual freedom. It also has to do with the fact that we still do not understand the nature of cooperation very well. Mutually beneficial relationships (known as mutualistic symbioses) clearly occur in nature, and they are especially important in social species such as humans. But from a theoretical, scientific perspective it is actually very hard to explain how they arise, and how they remain stable.
Fortunately, some progress has been made in recent decades that help us better understand both natural symbiosis and human cooperative structures. It seems that human cooperation is made possible by what we may call “morality”, combined with group identity markers and social structures that prevent or reduce antisocial behaviour.2 What’s more, it is possible to either undermine or strengthen the factors that scale up and stabilise cooperation. In other words, we are not doomed to failure in addressing collective problems, we do have some degree of (collective) choice in the matter.

To get a feeling for why cooperation and competition are such confusing subjects, consider the following questions:
First, what is the “true” nature of human relationships? We are said to be one of the most cooperative species, yet selfish and antagonistic behaviour seems widespread. Humans have a deep need for connection, love, friendship and belonging, yet the world remains plagued by corruption, conflict and exploitation—presumably outcomes few of us desire.

Second, our ability to cooperate in large groups seems to be one of humanity’s superpowers.3 It has enabled complex societies, science, rich and diverse cultures, art and advanced technology. This does present us with a puzzle: If cooperation is so powerful and an essential part of our nature, why did all experiments in communism quickly collapse into authoritarian states, propped up by corruption and oppression? After all, the idea behind communism is that common people will establish a cooperative utopia, once they are freed from the ruling classes that oppress and exploit them. Clearly this doesn’t seem to work so well in practice. And relatedly, why exactly is it so hard to fix large-scale coordination problems such as reducing pollution and global poverty, even if most people agree that this would be a good idea?

Finally, evolutionary theory tells us that competition is one of the driving forces behind the richness and complexity of nature. But if competition is so dominant, why is symbiosis and mutualism so common? Of course we do see much competition in nature, yet species also appear to aid one another. How do we explain this? Conversely, if collaboration seems widespread and a powerful force in nature, why are strong forms of cooperation so rare? Strong sociality (eusociality) is limited to only a handful of species, including ants, bees, termites and humans. These species have generally been extremely successful, so why isn’t sociality more common? The same is true for multicellularity, whereby once-independent cells cooperate to form complex organisms. This form of tight collaboration forms the basis for all complex life forms, but it evolved only a handful of times in the 4 billion year history of cellular life.4 How can something be both so successful and so rare?

Unfortunately our “human glasses” obscure the picture. We interpret the world through the lens of our own relationships, feelings and values. We assign moral weight to cooperation and competition. Depending on our values, beliefs and context, we may view either as good or bad, important or trivial. We might argue that cooperation within our own group is vital, but cooperation with opposing groups is immoral and should be avoided. We may claim that antisocial behaviour mostly occurs in “bad” people, who neither occur nor belong in our social group. Or we may argue that selfish behaviour is a sign of strength, while prosocial behaviour is naive and associated with weakness. We might assert that competition is good because it eliminates weakness or inefficiency and drives progress. Or that it is bad because it undermines the cooperation that sustains human success, as well as the effective functioning of organisations.

It isn’t hard to find reasons that help justify our beliefs about cooperation and competition. The problem is that these beliefs (and the associated ideologies) are often wrong, or at least they are too simplistic to capture a complex reality. In truth, we do not fully understand the nature of competition and cooperation, neither intuitively nor scientifically. Yet we often claim to know what we are talking about. For example, it is commonly argued that people are inherently selfish, competitive or even cruel. Such claims foster cynicism, which can erode trust and cooperation.5 They can also be used to explain and even justify extractive economic practices, inequality or oppression. But as Naomi Klein has pointed out, focusing on the supposed selfishness of human nature lets the systems that enable or encourage such behaviour off the hook. This is unfortunate, because while we cannot really change human nature, we can certainly change the systems that shape our behaviour.

In this article and the next, I hope to provide answers to some of the questions above. I aim to clarify some important aspects of cooperation and competition and human nature. I will explore the principles and processes that make ecosystems and cooperative human systems work, or that make them fragile or dysfunctional.

Theories about competition in Western thought date back at least to the 18th century and have profoundly shaped our worldview. Many people now think of economies, natural evolution and even history itself as being driven primarily by competition—between individuals, companies, nations, populations and species. The underlying idea is that life itself is a kind of competition, in which the strongest or most successful individuals or groups “win out” over the less successful. Over time, this leads to “survival of the fittest”, a process by which less successful groups (e.g. tribes, companies, societies, populations or species) disappear and are replaced by more successful ones.

The idea that life is a competition which one can either win or lose is intuitively attractive, but it is not necessarily correct. Game theory distinguishes between the various possible outcomes of games, conflicts or other competitive situations. A naive interpretation of natural selection, as described above, is known as a zero-sum game. We call it zero-sum because one party’s win amounts to another party’s loss. Zero-sum thinking is widespread, for instance in the worldview promoted by competitive “strongmen” like Donald Trump. It is also a well-known cognitive bias, because real-world situations often aren’t zero-sum games.
Even in competitive situations, game theory distinguishes two alternative outcomes: In a negative-sum or no-win situation, everyone ends up worse off. This is the case in destructive conflicts (all-out nuclear war being an extreme example), but also in much more common race-to-the-bottom scenarios.
And a third possible outcome is the positive-sum or win-win situation, common in (semi-)cooperative competitions. Cooperative competition sounds like a contradiction in terms, but that is just because we tend to think of competition and cooperation as mutually excluding opposites (which is actually an example of zero-sum bias). But a very well-known example of cooperative competition (or competitive cooperation) was already described in 1776, by the moral philosopher Adam Smith. In The Wealth of Nations, he described economic markets with a division of labour, and that thus feature a strong interdependence and degree of cooperation. There is also a degree of competition in such markets, of course, over finite necessities such as jobs and customers. But on the whole, Smith describes the economic market as a win-win situation, even if that outcome is largely unintentional. According to Smith, the merchant looks only for security and gain for himself, but the consequence is “to render the annual revenue of society as great as he can”.

In modern terms, we could call Smith’s economic market a “human ecosystem”, a diverse and adaptive complex system of interpersonal interactions, with an emergent outcome that is (to some extent) beneficial to everyone. The emergence of such a win-win outcome is not obvious from the nature of the interactions themselves, especially given their often competitive nature.
My comparison of human economies to natural ecosystems is no coincidence, and isn’t superficial. Granted, natural ecosystems tend to be a lot more competitive (and violent) than human economies. But in both kinds of systems there is a strong interdependence. Like humans in a diversified economy, species in an ecosystem depend on each other, for food as well as for many other “services”. And in both cases there is a degree of competition (e.g. species compete for food, space or mates). And while many of the interactions in an ecosystem are negative (think of individuals eating each other), the net emergent outcome is positive for all species involved, especially in the long term. A functional ecosystem provides resources to everyone and takes care of recycling them. It is necessary for the survival of most of its members and it is self-regulating to some extent, adapting to changes in conditions and composition. It drives the process of evolution. Yet nobody designed the system to do all these things, or takes care of managing it. Functional ecosystems emerge from interactions, many of which we would consider “negative”.

Charles Darwin famously described nature as being “red in tooth and claw”, reflecting the violent nature of natural interactions such as predation and competition. But his theory of natural selection, published in 1859, has also been widely misinterpreted as “proving” the importance of competitive strength.13 To see why this is a misinterpretation, we must understand the nature of “survival of the fittest”.7 Natural selection is actually better described as “the survival of the well fitted”.8 We speak of “selection” because it is associated with a reduced reproductive success (at least relatively speaking) of those that are less well fitted to their environmental circumstances. It is important to realise that “well fitted” is not the same as “strong”. And the “removal” of poorly fitted individuals from a population happens over the long term and is not necessarily an active process, or one brought about by competition.

There are many different ways in which one can be “fitted” to circumstances, and many of these actually involve a little help from others. Some of this help is quite direct: We humans and other animals get assistance from the micro-organisms in our microbiome, that help digest our food and protect us from disease.9 This is a good example of mutualistic symbiosis, a close relationship from which both parties derive clear benefit. But much of the help we receive is less direct. Plants don’t just provide us with food and oxygen, they also help regulate the local climate. They hold on to water and reduce extreme temperatures, thus making our direct environment more suitable for life and increasing our “fittedness”.10 And predators aren’t merely “nasty dangerous animals that kill”, they also help control parasites, pathogens and competitors, which indirectly benefits us and many others.11

There are also many ways in which one can be poorly fitted to the environment. If circumstances are extreme, it is actually hard to be well fitted. When conditions are very hot, cold or dry, very few species manage to survive. Those that manage to do so may actually face little competition.12 When conditions are better, competition may increase. Those that are relatively the best adapted, with or without the help of others, will eventually crowd out others. This shows that a poor environmental fit can also be due to our relations with others: the negative influence of competitors perhaps, but also of pathogens, parasites and predators, or simply a lack of positive relations with potential “helpers”.

Despite all this, when we think of natural selection, we mostly think of competition. In fact, Darwin himself emphasised the importance of competition13, for two main reasons. First, his work was inspired by Thomas Malthus, who highlighted the tension between population growth and limited resources. Malthus observed that populations expand exponentially, while resources often do not, potentially leading to scarcity and fierce competition. This insight led Darwin to think about the power of exponential reproduction and the restriction provided by resource constraints, which could be a natural force for selection.14 He came up with a conceptually simple theory that could explain life’s richness and adaptability, based on three core principles: natural variation, heritability and differential success. Darwin recognised that differential success could arise in ways other than through competition, but he also had a controversial theory to sell. This brings us to the second reason why he emphasised competition.
Darwin’s England was a highly competitive society, at the height of the Industrial Revolution. Adam Smith’s ideas on free markets had by then become widely accepted, reinforcing a belief in capitalism and competition as engines of progress. Framing competition as a key driver of evolution made Darwin’s theory more intuitive and palatable to the public, helping it gain acceptance.

After Darwin, theoretical work on both economics and evolutionary theory further established the importance of differential growth for success. In mathematical models of economies and populations it is easy to incorporate competition, or to get it out as a result.15 Such models typically assume simple relationships, limited diversity and static environments. Under such conditions, “survival of the fittest” generally guarantees success for those who grow fastest at others’ expense. This result is elegant, robust, and easy to grasp. The problem for theorists is that it also conflicts with much of what we see in the real world.

In the real world, competition does arise where shared resources are limited. Yet real organisms and species often have ways to reduce their competition with others, or to ameliorate its effects. They can relocate, switch resources, evolve and even coordinate with rivals or team up with potential helpers. Moreover, real environments are seldom static, so a competitive advantage today may become a disadvantage tomorrow. All this can greatly diminish the impact of competition. Unfortunately, these complexities are difficult to capture in elegant mathematical models. While more sophisticated models and simulations can demonstrate such effects, these are often hard to interpret or generalise.

Simple theories on the other hand remain attractive, especially if they promise progress or success. Economists love to emphasise the elegance of competitive markets, and biologists love to show how competition can contribute to adaptation. We continue to be rightly fascinated by how positive outcomes can result from interactions that, morally, we might view as negative.

The emphasis on competition in Western thought also has an ideological background. The prosperity of Western democracies is closely tied to capitalist economics and ideals of individual freedom and achievement. Especially during the Industrial Revolution and later the Cold War, individualism, freedom and competition were elevated to Western core values. Collectivism and central planning on the other hand became associated with communism, and were viewed with suspicion. In recent decades, however, the problems of free-market capitalism and individualism have come under renewed scrutiny. At the same time, our understanding of cooperation in nature has improved. As a result, symbiosis has become somewhat of a fashionable subject.

Let me first define what I mean by cooperation. For the purposes of this article, I consider behaviour in a relationship to be “cooperative” when it foregoes immediate self-interest in favour of a greater common gain. Cooperation therefore involves a cost, through investing time or resources, or simply from not pursuing maximum self-interest. But cooperation also has a benefit for everyone involved. In biology, such a relationship is known as “mutualism”, because the benefit is mutual. Symbiosis, however, is a broader concept.

Somewhat underwhelmingly, symbiosis simply means “living together”. While it often involves mutual benefit, it also encompasses more one-sided and extractive relationships, such as parasitism. Nevertheless, mutualistic relationships are widespread in nature. A familiar example is pollination and seed dispersal: plants provide animals with nectar, pollen, and edible seeds, while animals help plants recombine their genes and spread their offspring.
Another example is the relationship between animals and their gut microbes I mentioned earlier. Similar partnerships also exist between microorganisms and plants. Soil-dwelling fungi and bacteria help plants access essential nutrients, often in exchange for sugars produced through photosynthesis. If this sounds a little like economics, it is because it effectively is an underground economic market, with some dynamics and mechanisms strikingly similar to human economies.16

A further well-known example of symbiosis is agriculture. Though we often regard agriculture as a human invention, many ant species were already practising a form of farming millions of years before humans began domesticating plants and animals. Moreover, agriculture illustrates the ambiguity of symbiotic relationships. Factory farming, for instance, may appear purely extractive, yet we depend on food crops, and from a genetic perspective they depend on us. This has led some to suggest that certain grass species may have domesticated humans, rather than the other way around.17

What we can already see from these examples is that mutually beneficial symbiosis is common, but that the relationships are often ambiguous with regard to benefit and choice or control. In other words, it is often difficult to draw a line between benefit and exploitation, or between collaboration and dependence. This, again, is partly a result of our “human glasses”, the fact that we view the world through the lens of human feeling and morality. We tend to think of cooperation as inherently “good” and, perhaps more importantly, as something that needs to be a choice. The issue is that if cooperation is a choice, then not cooperating is also a choice. This is known as the free-rider problem: in a mutually beneficial relationship, there is usually a cost to providing benefits to others. It is therefore advantageous for individuals to receive benefits without reciprocating. This “cheating” strategy is so effective in the short-term that it tends to outcompete and undermine mutualistic cooperation, even though—“rationally”—everyone, including the cheaters, would benefit more from cooperation in the long term.

Unfortunately, the problem of cheating is both real and very persistent.18 As we shall see, it helps explain the inherent ambiguity in many symbiotic relationships. It also explains why large-scale social cooperation is rare in nature. Complex and intricate forms of cooperation are particularly vulnerable to cheating. Even ancient and successful cooperative systems still grapple with this issue, as seen in the occurrence of cancer in multicellular organisms like ourselves. Our cells clearly gain nothing from our death, yet our bodies must still need to go through great lenghts to prevent individual cells from cheating the collective and going rogue.19 Similar challenges arise in every collaborative relationship.

This again raises a crucial question: if cheating poses such a serious threat, how can strong forms of cooperation exist at all? After all, eukaryotic cells and multicellular organisms do exist. And while advanced sociality is rare, the success of ants, bees, and humans proves it is possible. It turns out that successful cooperation is indeed possible, and very powerful, but only if it manages to control the problem of cheating. In fact, there are several ways to achieve this.

All successful strategies for controlling cheating share a common principle. Remember that cooperation is vulnerable to cheating if cooperation is a choice, and therefore non-cooperation is also a choice. The key to robust cooperation therefore is to reduce the element of choice.

As we have seen, the mutual benefits of cooperation explain its success, but these benefits alone are not sufficient to prevent cheating, which in turn undermines cooperation. One highly effective approach is to increase the short-term cost of non-cooperation. Our bodies, for example, have immune systems that not only defend against pathogens but also detect and destroy potential cancer cells, at least most of the time. Similarly, all human societies have mechanisms to punish antisocial behaviour. The catch, of course, is that such enforcement itself requires cooperation. “Policing” can stablise cooperation, but it cannot explain how cooperation arises in the first place, or how it works in simpler settings. Yet it appears that all forms of cooperation do depend on restricting freedom, specifically, reducing the freedom to cheat or otherwise not work together.
This may seem rather obvious, but it is a fundamental insight that is easily overlooked.

In an earlier article on complexity, I introduced the principle of emergence, a concept I’ve already referenced above. Emergence involves the appearance of new behaviours and organisational structures from interactions among component parts. This results in “emergent levels of organisation” with properties that the individual components lack. A familiar example is the way economies emerge from human interactions, or ecosystems from interactions among organisms in general.

We often think of emergence as the addition of new behaviour and structure to a system. However, as Terrence Deacon and Jeremy Sherman argue, it may be more useful to view it as a process of removal and constraint. For new structural order to emerge, the component parts must lose some of their original degrees of freedom. If those parts retained complete freedom, it would be unlikely to yield any coherent order, let alone new, functional behaviour.20

All forms of emergence require some restriction of freedom for their components, enabling them to interact and generate functional behaviour rather than chaos. In an ecosystem, for example, organisms are not entirely free; they depend on and are constrained by others. Similarly, humans in a “free market” are not truly free—they follow laws, traditions, and moral norms, and rely on institutions.21 Without such constraints, the result would be disorder, not a functional economy that benefits its participants. Function demands some kind of order, and order requires some restriction of freedom.

For cooperative systems to work, this restriction of freedom must take a specific form: it must prevent any single member from benefiting too much at the expense of others in the relationship. The simplest way to achieve this (though not always sufficient) is through co-dependence, also known as obligate symbiosis. While this doesn’t fully explain how cooperation arises initially (where does the co-dependence come from?), it does help stabilise cooperation once it exists.22 After all, if two parties depend entirely on each other for survival, cheating on the relationship becomes far more difficult.

It is intuitively clear how either mutual dependence or “policing” to punish non-cooperation can prevent or limit cheating. Both mechanisms leave cooperators with little choice but to cooperate. Yet even under less restrictive conditions, nature exhibits stable, mutually beneficial cooperation that appears to arise from free choice. What stops either partner from exploiting the other?
In fact, wherever we observe a mutually beneficial relationship between non-dependent partners, the same interaction can often be described as “mutual parasitism”. Each party seeks to maximise its own benefit, but some power balance or external constraint keeps them in check.23

Consider the complex relationship between plants and mycorrhizal fungi. One could view this as mutual parasitism: fungi “infecting” plant roots to access sugars, or trees “domesticating” fungi to obtain more nutrients. Both perspectives have some merit, though neither is entirely accurate. In fact both plants and fungi coordinate with each other and have some control over their relationship. They can survive without each other and they can choose to withhold or reduce their contribution to the relationship. If one partner “cheats” (which occasionally happens), the other can respond by withdrawing its own contribution. In short, cheating carries negative consequences, which severely limits the potential success of cheaters. While cheating remains possible, a balance of power prevents one partner from gaining too much benefit at the other’s expense.24

Viewing a mutually beneficial relationship as “mutual parasitism” may seem a cynical take on cooperation. Yet it is only cynical from a human perspective, in which we tend to think of cooperation as a free and moral choice. This perhaps reveals more about the cooperative roots of human morality than about cooperation itself.
From a non-human standpoint, cooperation is a dependence relationship, which is more stable if there is less choice. It is beneficial in part because it unlocks new opportunities. To achieve this, however, it must restrict the freedom of all involved to act selfishly at others’ expense. This restriction then enables the emergence of cooperative systems that enrich the world and, in the long run, leave everyone better off.

In human societies, restricting selfish behaviour mainly means preventing the (excessive) exploitation of others or of “common goods”. How such restrictions are established, stabilised, and whether they are “fair” will be the subject of my next article.

I began this article by noting that cooperation and competition are difficult and often confusing subjects, illustrating this with several hard-to-answer questions. I will conclude by offering some answers.

Competition is not unimportant. We cannot understand ecosystems, economies, or evolution without invoking and understanding competition. But competition is also easy, in the sense that a competitive setting occurs wherever there are shared, limiting resources. Competition is therefore the baseline expectation in growing systems, which are always eventually limited by some resource such as space, energy or raw material.

What makes life remarkable is that it does not always take the easy path. Life is a constant struggle against decay and disorder, and thus an active, effortful process of organisation. It is therefore unsurprising that living beings have evolved ways to escape or mitigate the constraints of competition. Indeed, much of the richness of nature and human culture arises from this very effort.

Moreover, relationships in real, complex systems are rarely simple or mutually exclusive, and our categorisation of them is often vague and open to interpretation. In the real world, competition does not exclude cooperation, as can be clearly seen in human economies and societies. Even in nature, competition can occur together with coordination, dependence, exploitation and cooperation. In fact, this is what generates much of the diversity and complexity we see in natural ecosystems. If competition were the only game in town, nature would look very different.

Mutually beneficial symbioses are common in nature because they pay off. Cooperation creates win-win scenarios, whereas competition often leads to zero-sum or even lose-lose outcomes. Yet cooperation is always challenging, as it must contend with the problem of cheating. Although cooperation is often the best long-term solution for all, self-interested cheating tends to pay off for individuals in the short term. This is not an easy problem to solve, yet life has found ways to mitigate it to some extent, and so has human culture.

Stabilising cooperation requires limiting the freedom to act selfishly at the expense of others. Such restrictions can take many forms, including co-dependence, policing or mutual and external controls. More intimate and complex forms of cooperation demand stronger and more varied mechanisms to suppress cheating. As groups grow larger and more diverse, as in the evolution of human societies, cooperation becomes more difficult. Human cooperation relies on a combination of many stabilising mechanisms and structures. These include co-dependence, social feelings, trust, morality, punishment of cheating and several kinds of institutions. None of these are perfect, which is why human societies remain plagued by corruption, violence, inequality and “wicked” coordination problems., such as co-dependence, social bonds, trust, morality, punishment of cheating, and various institutions. Yet this does not mean we cannot improve the structures and mechanisms that underpin human cooperation. This is what I will explore in the next article.
